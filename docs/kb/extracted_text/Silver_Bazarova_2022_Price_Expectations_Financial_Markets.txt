Price Expectations for Financial Markets: Randomness and Signal Detection
Abstract
We examine contributions that noise can make to the objective of detecting signal in agent 
expectations for price in financial markets. Although contrary to most assumptions on exogenous noise in 
financial markets as increasing both risk and uncertainty in the detection of signal, a basis for the 
contribution that noise can have to agent objectives in signal detection through stochastic resonance (SR) 
is well-documented across disciplines. After reviewing foundations for the micro-processing of 
expectations, we offer a multicomponent model of networked agents that includes a component of bounded 
rational processing and a component that has been cited as generating “herding” behavior in financial 
markets. We then consider the effects of random disturbances that are common in financial markets on the 
detection of signal in expectations for price. The models that we implement in a signal-to-noise ratio directly 
investigate a process in stochastic resonance through which exogenous noise can increase efficiency in 
detecting signal. Results with both deterministic and stochastic forms of the proposed model support SR as 
a process in which randomness can contribute to the recovery of signal in agent expectation. We further 
report AUROC predictive models that indicate the sensitivity of the occurrence of SR to parameters of a 
model of agent expectations. 
Keywords: Price expectations, Signal detection in financial markets, Stochastic resonance, Multicomponent 
expectations, Networked agents.
1. INTRODUCTION
Agent expectations have long been established as intermediaries in market behavior across a range 
of disciplinary contexts (e.g., Arrow 1986, Arthur 2018, Bosse and Phillips 2016, Lucas, Mortenson, Siller, 
and Wallace 2013, Van Raaij 1989).  In financial economics, an increasing number of applications continue 
to indicate the relationship of measured expectations to market behavior in price and returns (e.g., 
Greenwood and Shleifer 2014; Sargent 2013). Accounts of the dynamics of market-related expectations,

commonly assume at least bounded rationality (e.g., Conlisk 1996) in the use of information to recover 
signal on price expectations. As now extensively addressed (e.g., Chen, Lux, and Marchesi, 2001), traders 
can be categorized as fundamentalists and “trend followers” or “noise” traders. Fundamentalists can be 
represented as at least bounded rational in the use of objective information in trading. So-called “noise” 
traders are commonly considered to be trend-followers or momentum traders. Seminal discourse that 
includes Grossman and Stiglitz (1980) and Black (1986) has established the importance of noise traders in 
the dynamics of market price. Also, see Peress and Schmidt (2021). In the absence of “noise” traders, 
market price would reduce to private and public information. 
Our interest is in extending the consideration of randomness in agent expectations for price in 
financial markets. Here we consider exogenous randomness as, for example, generated by currency 
devaluation, resource discovery and natural disasters. An agent is assumed to have the objective of detecting 
a signal for subsequent price in their processing of market-relevant information. In most models of 
expectations including those in which agents are categorized as fundamentalists or “noise” traders, 
exogeneous randomness can be assumed to increase uncertainty and risk. We will give a form to and 
investigate a condition in which exogenous randomness can increase agent efficiency in detecting the signal 
in price dynamics. 
1.1 Research Problem and Objectives. Agent expectations have been theorized to efficiently anticipate 
price in financial markets (e.g., Greenwood and Shleifer 2014; Lucas, Mortensen, Shiller and Wallace 2013; 
Sargent 2013). Our research objective is to increase our understanding of dynamics in agent expectations 
for price in financial markets through (1) elaborations of behavioral foundations of micro-processing in 
these dynamics, (2) integration of conditions of SR under which the exogenous randomness that is endemic 
in financial markets can increase efficiency in the recovery of signal and (3) testing the sensitivities of the 
occurrence of SR to model parameters. We report results for the research objectives in the estimation of 
what is the signature of SR in the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of multicomponent models of expectations.

2. MULTICOMPONENT AGENT-BASED MODELS IN FINANCIAL MARKETS
Multicomponent models of agents in financial markets typically include a component in the 
processing of other agents that influence the agent and generate what is commonly cited as “herding” in 
financial markets (e.g., Chen, Tan, and Zheng, 2015; Demirer and Kutan, 2006). Harras and Sornette (2011) 
offer a multi-component model of adaptive agents for the growth of a “bubble”. In their model, agents 
switch from buyers to sellers or vice-versa, through exogenously driven "news-based" adjustments to the 
weights of the components. In exercises that these investigators report, news sources appear to increase the 
correlation between agents in their states and presage or are coordinate with the growth of a price “bubble”. 
In a similar direction, Eckrot, Jurczyk, and Morgenstern (2016) investigate a multi-component 
agent-based model that allows for a case of multiple assets and information for which there is either public 
or private access. Their model is cited as an Ising analog and shows properties of fat tails and volatility 
clustering that have long been recognized in financial markets. Although, not strictly in a multi-component 
model, Li and Teo (2021) have considered a combinatorial form for uncertainty and randomness in financial

markets under an alternative definition of skewness in the randomness. The investigators integrate their 
definition of randomness in a modified mean-variance model. 
While we agree with previous investigators that market behavior in aggregates is likely to be 
generated by contrasts in agent processing that introduce different dynamics, we extend other recent 
investigations by proposing multiple components in the processing of individual agents. Rather than 
grouping traders in categories of fundamentals and “noise” traders that have different dynamics, our 
multicomponent models of agent processing generate price expectations in financial markets from 
competition between components in bounded rational processing and mimetic or “copying” of other agents, 
i.e., “herding”. The non-linear dynamics of a transition function that defines the relative weight of each 
component reflects agent behavior in response to a distance between market price and metrics of 
fundamental value. We next elaborate on behavioral foundations for the micro-processing in expectations 
for price that we propose.
2.1 A Multicomponent Model of Agent Heuristics in the Formation of Expectations:
2.1.1 Bounded Rational Processing. In agreement with a range of specifications of expectations, we 
recognize that agents do generally process objective information on the market in a form that is consistent 
with rationality (e.g., Sargent, 2013) or what is at least bounded rationality (e.g., Conlisk 1996, Kahneman 
2003, Simon 1972). Theorists of bounded rationality recognize that agents are limited in their information 
processing capabilities and commonly use approximation heuristics in this processing. In a representation 
of this component, we assume that agents use predictive information on fundamentals of value that underlie 
market price (e.g., price-to-earnings or price-earnings to growth ratios x company earnings) in forming their 
expectations but adjust their expectations based on feedback from the past accuracy of comparable 
information in predicting price.
2.1.2 Imitative Processing and Herding. While there are contentions that even bounded rationality can 
maintain efficient markets (e.g., Lo 2007), behavior that exhibits so-called “excessive exuberance” (Shiller 
2015) and the copying of the expectations of others is widely recognized as departing from strict rationality

and generating “herding” in the expectations of agents (e.g., Rangvid, Schmeling and Schrimpf 2013). 
Chen, Tan, and Zheng (2015) have investigated an agent-based model in which “herding” exists at levels 
of individual securities, market sectors, and overall market levels. Consistent with these and other 
investigators, we give a form to imitative processing in a second component of agent expectations for price 
that directly follows from agent observations of the states or levels of referent individuals or a proxy for 
these levels. 
The economic importance that an equivalent of imitative processing can have to expectations has 
been cited in different disciplines (Barsky, Kimball, and Smith 2012; Paul, Cuthill, Kuroso, Norton, 
Woodgate, and Mendl 2011; Klaaren, Hodges and Wilson 1994). In an extreme, qualitative texts on 
expectations report a range of instances in which agents distinctly depart from rational or bounded rational 
processing (e.g., Kindleberger 1978; Smith, Suchanek, and Williams 1988). Such a so-called “mercurial” 
sensitivity with which “herding” can build has been observed across many applications in financial markets 
(e.g., Devenow and Welch 1996; Demirer and Kutan 2006; Zhang and Liu 2012). 
2.1.3 Transition Function. We next elaborate on a behavioral basis for dynamic variation in the relative 
weights of bounded rational and herding components of agent expectations. Component dominance in the 
transition function that we propose is defined by a distance of market price from metrics of fundamental 
value (e.g., historical price to earnings (PE) or PE to growth ratios (PEG)). The transition function allows 
for what we consider to be the more realistic agent behavior of one component dominating with continuing 
processing occurring in the other component. The transition function we propose further introduces non-
linear dynamics in price expectations since extreme departures of market price from metrics of fundamental 
value that are driven by the equivalent of “herding” are commonly “corrected” by what can be an abrupt 
transition to a level that is closer to fundamental value metrics. 
Non-Linear Dynamics in the Transition Function. In the agent processing that we represent, component 
dominance that is dominated by imitative processing can revert to bounded rational processing to correct 
for a price level of an asset relative to its fundamental value that an agent judges to be “excessive”. A basis 
for the non-linear dynamics that we introduce in the transition function is implied by a number of

3. STOCHASTIC RESONANCE (SR)
The indirect and subtle ways that randomness can contribute to functional objectives have been 
engagingly recognized in qualitative discourse by authors that include Gladwell (2006) and Taleb (2005). 
In applications to financial markets, a basis for a well-defined account in which exogenous randomness can 
contribute to the detection of signal can be through stochastic resonance (SR: McDonnell, Stocks, Pearce, 
and Abbott 2008). In SR, a level of exogenous randomness has been shown to have the capability to make 
a sub-threshold signal detectible. The generality of SR is now supported by extensive numerical and 
empirical applications across disciplines that include Hopfield neural networks (e.g., Duan, Duan, Chapeau-
Blondeau and Abbott 2020; Hopfield 2006; Šíma and Orponen 2003), predator signals in crayfish (e.g., 
Wiesenfeld and Moss 1995), and perceptual interpretation of ambiguous figures (e.g., Harmer, Davis and 
Abbott 2002; Riani and Simonotto 1994) as well as binary states of financial markets in “bubbles and 
crashes” (e.g., Krawiecki and Holyst 2003; Zhou, Zhong, Li, Li, and He 2018). 
Available applications of SR in financial markets have not addressed price expectations of agents 
and the multiple components of bounded rationality (e.g., Conlisk, 1996) and the influence of referent 
agents on an agent (e.g., Baddeley 2010; Spyrou 2013; Zhang and Liu 2012) in their processing. Since 
expectations are precursors of market behavior, there is an opportunity to contribute to our understanding 
of dynamics in financial markets by extending the condition of exogenous randomness in the detection of 
signal in expectations. 
Our specification of agent expectations offers a more complete representations of continuous agent micro-
processing in financial markets than has been introduced in previous demonstrations of SR in binary states 
of “bubbles” and “crashes” in market price (e.g., Krawiecki and Holyst, 2003; Zhou, Zhong, Li, Li, and He,

2018). The results we report demonstrate the functionality that exogenous randomness can have under 
certain conditions in financial markets through SR in elaborated models of agent expectations for price. 
The range of applications across disciplines that have demonstrated the case of efficiency-increasing 
randomness does not ensure a comparable effect in the detection of signal for market price in agent 
expectations. However, the results of the background studies we cite encourage the direct investigation of 
the case in expectations for market price. 
3.1 Randomness as Increasing Efficiency in the Detection of Signal. The information theoretic tradition 
that follows from Shannon (e.g., Shannon and Weaver 1949; also see Cover and Thomas 2006) investigates 
the objective of recovering signal in the presence of noise. This is clearly an objective of agents in financial 
markets (Scheffer, Bascompte, et al 2009; De Long, Shleifer, Summers, and Waldmann 1990). This 
objective is commonly given a form in the signal-to-noise ratio. A range of investigations have elaborated 
and tested an account of stochastic resonance in which noise of a certain intensity can contribute to rather 
than detract from the detection of signal (McDonnell, Stock, Pearce, and Abbott, 2008).  
3.2 Dynamics in Stochastic Resonance. Accounts of SR introduce a basis to expect that even the “weak 
periodic variation” that environments impose in price expectations can be made more detectable by 
randomness. Stochastic resonance is perhaps most easily understood in a case where the system’s variation 
is not directly observable except in terms of “switching” events (i.e., a change of states as in “on-off” states).  
In a simple case of demonstrated SR, deterministic variation is often given weak periodic (e.g., sinusoidal) 
variation as signal. Here, random variation, as in Gaussian “noise” can be added to each agent to represent 
agent idiosyncrasy in signal. Noise can increase the impulse of the periodic signal and “switch” the state. 
Thus, the combination of a certain level of noise and signal can be more informative on the state of the 
system than signal is in the absence of noise. This is schematically represented in Figure 2.
In Figure 2, signal by itself does not attain the minimum magnitude at which an agent can detect it 
as shown in the “no noise” and “weak noise” panels. The addition of an optimal Gaussian noise level that

The signal-to-noise ratio remains the common metric for detecting efficiency in the recovery of signal in 
information theory (e.g., Cover and Thomas 2006). In applications to SR, the presence of SR is typically 
indicated by (1) an identifiable maximum in the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) as a function of noise intensity 
in the time domain and (2) a corresponding maximum for the Fourier Transform in the frequency domain. 
The explicit form given the SNR is dependent on the application and differs across applications that range 
from ocean engineering (e.g., Hari, Anand, Premkumar, and Madhukumar 2009) to opinion dynamics (e.g., 
Tessone and Toral 2005) and financial and energy markets (e.g., Dong, Wen, Hu, and Li 2020; Krawiecki 
and Hołyst 2003; Zhou, Zhong, Li, Li, and He 2018). 
As we have noted, the direct demonstration of noise-induced enhancement of signal in nonlinear 
systems (e.g., McDonnell, Stocks, Pearse, and Abbott 2008; Ward, Neiman, and Moss 2002) does not 
ensure commensurate effects in agent expectations for market price. Available demonstrations of SR across 
contextual representations do encourage explicit applications of the process in economic agents. In the 
section that follows, we will review applications of SR in financial markets. Following this review, we will

3.3 Applications of Stochastic Resonance in Financial Markets. Xiao-Ming, Kai, and Qi (2002) have 
examined SR in a model of the binary states of “bubbles” and “crashes” that follows Kiselev, Phillips, and 
Gabitov (2000) where the interest rate is considered as an external signal and the randomness in traders’ 
behavior is noise. Their results indicate the presence of SR that increases with noise intensity to a maximum. 
Krawiecki and Holyst (2003) investigated a bi-stable model of a financial market designed to represent 
“bubbles and crashes” as subject to a weak external information-carrying signal and noise. These authors 
show that an information-carrying signal assumed to be too weak to induce the equivalent of “magnetization 
jumps” can be enhanced by external noise as in standard SR accounts. Helbing and Platkowski (2000) and 
Krawiecki and Holyst (2003) implement a form in which the arrival of information considered to have 
relatively weak importance to an objective can be enhanced by external noise as in stochastic resonance 
and result in the end to a "bubble” and a market “crash”.
Zhou, Zhong, Li, Li, and He (2018) have implemented a modified Heston model of market price 
and directly demonstrated SR in the periodic volatility of two financial markets. Their experimental results 
demonstrate that (1) optimal levels of volatility parameters can maximize the effects of both systematic and 
non-systematic randomness on market periodicity and (2) different correlation magnitudes between 
systematic and non-systematic noise can result in different critical points that can induce single or multiple 
instances of SR.
Although not directly evidenced in stochastic resonance, the importance to price that noise and drift 
(e.g., Black and Scholes, 1973) can have in evolutionary games has also been recognized (Vega-Redondo 
1996). Vukov, Szabó, and Szolnoki (2006) have directly identified SR as generating cooperation in a 
Prisoner's Dilemma game. Less frequently, SR has been applied to the study of opinion formation 
(Kuperman and Zanette 2002; Tessone and Toral 2005) that the authors note as having analogs to binary 
states in market behavior.

  4. INVESTIGATING STOCHASTIC RESONANCE IN THE DYNAMICS OF 
MULTICOMPONENT EXPECTATIONS
A component of a multi-component model of “herding” in a model of expectations is most 
informedly investigated in network models since agents at collective levels exhibit structural 
configurations. We study models of agent expectations when agents are integrated in small-world networks 
(SWN: Watts and Strogatz 1998). For integration of SWNs in economic analysis also see Jackson 
2010;2014). SWNs parametrize structure in a network as a proportion () of the total vertices in the network 
that are disconnected from neighbors or next neighbors in a regular network and re-connected to a randomly 
selected vertex. This structural parameter has been related to path length (mean number of transfers across 
vertices to connect any two vertices in the network) and clustering in states or levels of variables in the 
network. 
Relatively small proportions of total connections in the SWN that are to a remote vertex (e.g., ρ ~ 
.001) have been shown to have highly significant effects on path length and clustering in the network (Barrat 
and Weigt 2000). Path length and clustering have direct interpretability in economic analyses. On path 
length and economic efficiency in networks, see Cowan and Jonard (2004), and Wen, Yang, and Zhou 
(2019). On clustering in networks and economic inequality, see D'Angelo and Lilla (2011), Tsvetkova, 
Wagner, and Mao (2018), Brida, Carrera, and Segarra (2020). In the exercises to follow, we directly 
investigate the relationship of the ρ parameter in SWNs in conjunction with other parameters to define 
efficiency and structure in the detection of signal in agent expectations.

distribution of all agents in the networks that we have defined. We integrate market data to operationalize 
the models and enact a direct search algorithm that iterates over defined ranges of parameters to instantiate 
a universe of estimates of expectations. We then examine the occurrence of SR as a function of χ, where χ 
is the noise level in the time and frequency domains in these estimates. 
Elaborating on standard definitions of SR, we define its incidence in the time and frequency 
domains as a spike of at least twice the magnitude of the maximum of any observation of the SNR sequence 
we generate. Given demonstrations of SR in the defined models of the SNR, we will then examine estimates 
and graphics of predictive models of SR in the SNR over defined model parameters with AUROC

7. SUMMARY and DISCUSSION
Following a review of expectations as precursors of price levels in financial markets, we have 
introduced a multicomponent model of price expectations. In the multicomponent model, we represent a 
component in bounded rational processing and a component that represents the influence of other agents 
on an agent’s expectations and generates commonly-cited “herding” in financial markets. We further 
specify a transition function for component dominance that depends on the distance between market price 
and metrics of fundamental value. A behavioral basis for transitions in component dominance has been 
elaborated in support of the form given in the specification of this function. Following a background review 
of multicomponent models of expectations, we have defined an agent objective in the detection of signal 
on the direction and level of expectations for market price. 
While in most models of expectations, exogenous randomness is assumed to interfere with 
detecting the equivalent of signal for market price levels, we review conditions in which randomness can 
facilitate the detection of signal. Stochastic resonance has been elaborated as a general process through 
which randomness can increase efficiency in the objective of detecting signal across a wide range of 
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=4149256

--- PAGE 27 ---
26
applications. In subsequent sections, we defined SR in a signal-to-noise ratio for expectations and directly 
investigated whether alternative operationalizations of SR in the multi-component models of expectations 
we propose can evidence increases in the detection of signal through levels of randomness. 
In a deterministic model of the SNR, we use data on the S&P index for market price, mean earnings, 
and historical PE ratios of the companies in the index to operationalize fundamental value in the model. In 
a stochastic model, we specify a derived price following historical representations of price dynamics in 
short intervals as Brownian motion with drift. As indicated in Eq. (1) and (3) of the general model of the 
deterministic model, and Eq. (8) of the stochastic model, the difference between market price and 
fundamental value defines component weights through a proposed transition function. 
In exercises with both models, we implement small-world networks (SWN) models to represent 
structure in agent interactions within a component that generates “herding” in expectations. We initially 
used a direct search algorithm in C++ code to identify instances of SR in the parameter spaces of the signal-
to-noise ratios of defined models. Our results with both of the models we implement support SR as a process 
through which noise can contribute to the detection of signal in financial markets.
We have then reported AUROC predictive models of the discrimination of SR over the defined 
parameter ranges of model parameters for both the deterministic and stochastic models. Across all 
estimations of both the models, parameters of network structure in the SWN () and the magnitude of inter-
agent influence (Mij) in the component that generates “herding” were indicated to be among the significant 
predictors of SR. Taken together, our results provide strong support for the capability of the proposed 
system to accommodate SR in the recovery of signal on expectations for price in financial markets. 
These results, in turn, further indicate the importance that randomness can have to efficient markets. 
The recovery of signal on price is a common objective in a range of applications to energy, financial, and 
commodity markets. In most market applications, as in most engineering applications, the objective has 
been in recovering signal in cases where noise obscures the signal. Here, as in applications in a range of 
disciplines that have been cited, there is a basis to anticipate that under certain conditions, randomness in 
expectations for financial markets can increase rather than detract from efficiency in agent objectives of

detecting signal. Our results add to evidence that cases in which noise can contribute to the recovery of the 
signal are likely to be more general than had previously been recognized in these markets. 
The contribution that randomness can make to detecting signal under certain conditions does not 
necessarily mean that one can “tune” a representation of randomness to increase efficiency in the detection 
of signal on market price (McDonnell, Stocks, et al, 2008). In market applications as in other applications, 
randomness is embedded in structural determinations of price. It does further support the indication that the 
extensive treatment of randomness as solely decreasing efficiency in the detection of signal on price in 
financial markets can be modified for the case of stochastic resonance. We extend models of binary states 
of financial markets to a case of continuous processing in multi-component expectations.
Finally, we take note of several directions for the discourse on models of expectations and their 
implementation in financial markets that follow from the models we implement. First, we note that contrary 
to an alternative perspective in natural selection that others have applied to economic institutions (e.g., 
Alchian 1950; Cosmides and Tooby 1994), it can be a sufficient condition for efficiency-increasing 
randomness to have been coordinated with other processes in dynamic systems that increased efficiency in 
signal detection. Once in place, efficiency-increasing noise can become structurally integrated in dynamics 
and remain operative. The basis for such an observation can be found in a number of examples of sensory 
and physical processes. See, for example, “hitchhiking” in evolution (e.g., Barton 2000). 
Second, we note that there are alternatives in representations of how agents converge to levels of 
noise that maximize an SNR. As now well documented, self-organization (e.g., Kauffman, 1993; Zhou and 
Sornette, 2007) can allow convergence to an equilibrium from semi-autonomous behavior (i.e., behavior 
that uses greatly simplified rules to coordinate with other agent’s expectations) that results in organized 
behavior at a group level (e.g., Elsner 2017; Foster and Metcalfe 2003; Lux 1995). In well-recognized 
examples in studies of flocking (e.g., Choi, Ha and Li 2017; Ha, Jeong, Noh, Xiao and Zhang 2017), entities 
in their studies commonly use rules to organize that are simple for agents, (e.g., distance from others), but 
have emergent properties at the group level that benefit individual entities such as resistance to predators. 
As such, there may be a basis to examine corresponding applications of “swarm-based” coordination of the

--- PAGE 29 ---

expectations of agents in financial markets that can contribute to the efficiency that noise can have in signal 
detection.
Third, conditions in environments of financial markets can mediate the existence of SR and the 
level of noise that maximizes the recovery of signal (e.g., Fase and Abma 2003; Xu and Zhang 2009).  In 
financial markets, “turbulence” in the economy or an influential sector can constitute environmental factors 
in market dynamics. A number of investigators have observed that consensus and accuracy in prediction 
of market price commonly trade-off in properties of signal recovery when multi-agent interaction is 
involved (e.g., Elton, Gruber, and Gultekin 1981). Consensus can be in terms of minimizing inter-agent 
variability to facilitate convergence in the level of model parameters that maximize the SNR. Accuracy is 
necessarily related to discriminating the SNR-maximizing level of noise in consensus estimates. 
In market expectations, the stability or variability of exogenous environments in which signal 
recovery occurs can underlie consensus and accuracy in defining a noise level that best contributes to 
efficiency in signal recovery. A low level of exogenous noise may be most advantageous to consensus in 
extracting signal under relatively stable environments since variability in inter-agent differences under this 
condition is likely to be low. In contrast, an increased level of exogenous noise may be most advantageous 
to consensus and extracting signal in unstable environments. 
8. CONCLUSIONS
The importance of agent expectations to understanding dynamics of financial markets continues to 
be demonstrated by a range of investigators. Multicomponent models remain conceptually and empirically 
relevant in the study of expectations for price in financial markets The results that we report with 
multicomponent models contribute to the evidence that stochastic resonance, as initially demonstrated in 
biological and physical systems (McDonnell, Stocks, et al 2008; Nozaki, Collins and Yamamoto 1999; 
Wiesenfeld and Moss 1995) is a much more general mechanism that can have an important function in 
detecting signal in financial markets. Whereas background studies we cite have reported SR in market 
cycles of “bubbles and crashes”, we have examined SR in continuous, multi-component models of price

expectations. Our results for both deterministic and stochastic models suggest that under certain conditions 
the randomness that is endemic to financial markets can contribute rather than detract from the recovery of 
signal in agent expectations. The information-theoretic framework that we reference in defining the SNR 
can provide a useful starting point to investigate the mechanisms of SR and their generality in markets. 
From the above, processing in multicomponent models for signal from expectations and the study of 
conditioning variables that include randomness continue to be directions that contribute to our 
understanding of the dynamics of financial markets.
