Analyst Forecast Revisions and Market Price Formation  
 
Abstract 
We document several factors that help explain cross-sectional variations in the delayed 
price response to individual analyst forecast revisions.  First, the market does not make a 
sufficient distinction between those analysts providing new information and others 
simply “herding” toward the consensus.  Second, the market responds more completely to 
“celebrity” analysts, and under-weights revisions by obscure, but highly accurate, 
analysts.  Third, controlling for firm size, the market price adjustment is more complete 
for firms with wider analyst coverage.  Moreover, a significant portion of the delayed 
price response is corrected around future earnings news events, particularly forecast 
revisions by other analysts.  Taken together, these findings show that qualitative aspects 
of an earnings signal can affect the speed and efficacy of the price formation process.

I.  INTRODUCTION 
This study examines the information content and market price formation process 
associated with individual analyst earnings forecast revisions.  Our primary goal is to 
better understand the characteristics of forecast revisions that affect their immediate 
market impact, as well as the magnitude of the subsequent price drift over the next two 
years.   We are particularly interested in how these characteristics affect the proportion of 
the total price response that is observed immediately, as opposed to the delayed price 
response which appears in the following months. 
 
Forecast revisions of individual analysts play an important role in the dissemination of 
corporate earnings.  Unlike quarterly earnings announcements, individual analyst forecast 
revisions take place throughout the quarter.  Because of their frequency and timeliness, 
these revisions have become a vital source of information for many users of corporate 
financial reports.  Web-based technology that delivers individual forecast revisions to 
investors in real-time is now widely available, elevating their usefulness.1  As access to 
this information increases, the need to better understand the information content of each 
individual revision increases. 
 
Our study is also motivated by the on-going debate on market efficiency, particularly as it 
pertains to earnings news.2  The delayed market response to earnings news releases is 
perhaps the single most perplexing anomaly that has emerged from accounting-based 
capital market research over the past 20 years. 3  The empirical fact that firms with 
positive (negative) earnings news subsequently experience positive (negative) abnormal 
returns is a matter of interest to both accounting academics and financial practitioners.

But while this empirical regularity is well documented, surprisingly little is known about 
qualitative factors associated with an earnings signal that can either hinder, or aid, the 
market in assimilating this information.   
 
In this study, we nominate, and test, several qualitative attributes of individual forecast 
revisions that we expect will affect the speed and efficacy of the price formation process.  
Specifically, we test the conjecture that the delayed market reaction to analyst forecast 
revisions is related to: a.) whether an analyst is bringing new information or is simply 
“herding” toward the consensus forecast (a signal attribute); b.) whether he or she is an 
“All-Star” analyst (an analyst attribute); and c.) whether the firm is being covered by a 
large number of analysts (a firm attribute).   
 
Our results show that all three characteristics are related to the subsequent price 
formation process.  First, we find that the delay in price response to earnings news is 
more pronounced for a higher quality (“confirming”) signal, defined as a forecast 
revision that is in the same direction with respect to both an analyst’s own prior forecast 
and the prior consensus forecast.4  Second, we find that forecasts made by “celebrity” 
analysts (Institutional Investor All-Stars) elicit a stronger immediate price response, but a 
less pronounced subsequent price drift.  In contrast, the subsequent price drift is more 
pronounced for revisions by superior analysts who are more likely to be from smaller 
firms (WSJ Earnings Estimators).  Third, controlling for other attributes, we show that 
the post-revision price drift is more pronounced in firms with lower analyst coverage.   
 
To ensure our results are not driven by differences in risk characteristics across firms, we 
control for firm size, B/M ratio, and price momentum.  In addition, we show that a 
significant portion of the delayed price reaction occurs around the next four earnings

announcements.  Interestingly, we find an even greater portion of the delayed reaction is 
observed in the short-window returns around the next six forecast revisions.  This 
evidence suggests that the market is adjusting slowly to the information contained in 
individual forecast revisions, and that later revision by other analysts act as catalysts in 
the price formation process. 
 
These findings point to several imperfections in the price discovery process for earnings 
revisions.  First, the market does not appear to make a sufficient distinction between 
analysts who are unambiguously providing new information, and those who are simply 
“herding” toward the consensus.  Second, the market seems to pay more attention to 
analysts that have acquired “celebrity” status, but is more likely to under-appreciate 
revisions made by more obscure analysts with superior forecasting ability.  Finally, the 
price drift is lower for firms with greater analyst coverage, suggesting that the existence 
of multiple analysts helps facilitate the price discovery process.  Taken together, these 
findings suggest a market that does not quickly incorporate the more subtle, or more 
qualitative, aspects of an earnings signal. 
 
Our study is related to several strands of recent research.  First, it extends the growing 
literature on the role of analysts as information intermediaries.  Prior studies find 
evidence that the level of financial analyst coverage affects the efficiency with which the 
market processes information (Brennan et al. (1993), Walther (1997), Bhattacharya 
(2001), and Elgers, et al. (2001)).  We show that, in the case of analyst forecast revisions, 
increased analyst coverage generally results in a lower subsequent price drift, suggesting 
that analysts help to facilitate price discovery.   
 
Second, our results highlight the usefulness of analyst information in investment 
decisions.  Elgers et al. (2001) show that a simple trading strategy based on the ratio of 
price to one-year-ahead earnings estimates (P/FY1) yields significant positive abnormal 
returns.  Similarly, several recent studies (e.g., Womack (1996), Jegadeesh et al. (2001)) 
find that analyst recommendation revisions predict returns over the next 3 to 9 months.

Our findings suggest that the simple forecast revision strategy suggested by Stickel 
(1991) can be enhanced by considering the quality of the revision signal, the amount of 
analyst coverage in a firm, and, to a lesser extent, the identity of the analyst. 
 
Finally, our results shed light on the price discovery mechanism, and help define the 
boundaries of market efficiency.  Much of the regulatory debate in accounting standard 
setting revolves around the issue of “transparency” – that is, whether investors can 
readily decipher more subtle aspects of financial reports.  Our findings suggest that 
certain qualitative aspects of an earnings signal can be problematic for market 
participants.   
 
The “salience” of a signal (as measured by an analyst’s reputation), the “quality” of the 
signal itself (whether an analyst is herding toward the consensus), and the amount of 
analyst coverage a firm receives (perhaps an indicator of capacity constraints in the 
analysis of earnings news), can all affect the speed of the price adjustment process for 
earnings news.  Taken together, our findings suggest that the process by which earnings 
signals are incorporated into price should not be taken for granted, and that it deserves 
greater attention from accounting researchers and standard setters. 
 
 
II. RELATED LITERATURE AND HYPOTHESES DEVELOPMENT 
 
A. Motivation and related literature 
Two main stylized facts emerge from prior research on analyst forecast revisions.   First, 
these forecast revisions are price informative, in that they are associated with a significant 
immediate market response at the time of their release.  Second, the immediate price 
response is incomplete, in that prices tend to continue to drift in the same direction for at 
least 3 to 9 months after the price revision.  
 

--- PAGE 7 ---
 
 
 
6
 
Evidence that analyst forecasts are price informative dates back to the 1970s.  Early 
research by Griffin (1976), Givoly and Lakonishok (1979, 1980) and Imhoff and Lobo 
(1984) documents the market response to analyst forecast revisions.  In fact, Elton, 
Gruber and Gultekin (1981) show that foreknowledge of analyst revisions is more value-
relevant than foreknowledge of the reported earnings themselves.  Collectively, these 
findings show that analyst forecast revisions bring new information to the market.   
 
At least three subsequent studies document the fact that the short-window price reaction 
around forecast revisions is incomplete.  Using a small sample, Givoly and Lakonishok 
(1980) first reported a post-announcement drift after earnings forecast revisions.  
Extending this line of inquiry, Stickel (1991) demonstrated that firms whose consensus 
forecast have been recently revised upwards (downwards) tend to earn positive (negative) 
abnormal returns over the next 3 to 12 months.  Finally, Chan, Jegadeesh, and 
Lakonishok (1996) confirm Stickel’s finding and show that it is part of a general class of 
“momentum” strategies, in which the market response to recently released information is 
incomplete.5 
 
The focus of our study is on factors that either exacerbate, or mitigate, the delayed price 
reaction to analyst forecast revisions.  Whereas prior studies focus on changes in the 
consensus analyst forecast, we examine the market response to individual analyst 
revisions.  The main advantage of this approach is that it allows us to separately evaluate 
attributes of the information event associated with firm characteristics, analyst identity, 
and the revision signal itself.  We thus extend prior research by documenting differences 
in the delayed price adjustment process for forecasts with differing firm, analyst, and 
signal attributes.

(1989, 1990), who find a price drift after earnings surprises, and Womack (1996), who documents a price 
drift after analysts’ buy/sell recommendations.  In each case, the evidence points to an immediate short-
window price response to the information release, followed by a generally sluggish adjustment process in 
the same direction.

B. Hypothesis development 
B.1 Signal Attributes 
First, we examine aspects of the signal itself that could affect the market’s ability to fully 
assimilate the information contained in a revision.  Analyst forecasts are typically 
reported along with the analyst's prior forecast and the most recent consensus forecast.  
This fact suggests that both the analyst's own prior forecast and the consensus forecast 
are potentially important benchmarks in assessing the information content of the revision.  
 
Our measure of signal attribute considers the direction of the forecast revision relative to 
both the prior consensus and the analyst's own prior forecast (see Figure 1).  Forecasts 
that move up (down) relative to both the analyst's own prior forecast and the prior 
consensus are labeled confirming good-news (bad-news) signals.  The direction of a 
confirming signal relative to both benchmarks suggests unambiguously that the analyst is 
bringing new information to the market. 
 
Forecasts that are between the analyst's own prior forecast and the prior consensus are 
labeled conflicting signals (Figure 1).  In the case of a conflicting signal, whether the 
revision indicates good or bad news depends on whether the revision is compared to the 
analyst's own prior forecast or the prior consensus. When an analyst issues a conflicting 
signal, (s)he is exhibiting a tendency to “herd” towards the consensus.  An earlier finding 
by Stickel (1990) suggests that conflicting forecasts likely contain less information than 
confirming forecasts.  Specifically, Stickel (1990) shows that changes in the consensus 
are useful in predicting the next revision of any given analyst.  This result implies that 
conflicting signals are probably less informative (regardless of magnitude), because they 
simply reflect information revealed by the prior revisions of other analysts. 
 
We hypothesize that confirming forecast revisions are more informative than conflicting 
forecasts, and that a confirming signal will be associated with a greater immediate price 
impact.  To the extent that the market does not make a sufficient qualitative distinction

between those analysts that are herding and those that bring new information to market, 
we also expect confirming signals to be associated with a greater subsequent price drift. 
 
B.2 Analyst Attributes 
The identity of the analyst is another characteristic of forecast revisions that can affect 
both the information content of the signal and the efficiency of the price discovery 
process.  Early research found no evidence of systematic performance differences among 
analysts in terms of the accuracy of their forecasts (e.g., Brown and Rozeff 1980, O'Brien 
1990, and Butler and Lang 1991).  However, Stickel (1992) uses more comprehensive 
controls for the age of the forecasts and shows that analysts on the Institutional Investor 
All-American team provide more accurate forecasts.  In the same spirit, and using a large 
sample that spans many industries, Sinha, Brown, and Das (1997) show that certain 
analysts do consistently outperform others in terms of the accuracy of their forecasts.  
 
Subsequent studies have focused on the economic determinants of individual analyst 
forecast accuracy, such as analyst experience (Mikhail et al. 1997), analyst abilities, 
resources and portfolio complexity (Clement 1999), analyst aptitude and brokerage house 
characteristics (Jacob et al. 1999), and age of the forecast (Brown and Mohammad 2001). 
Brown (2001) demonstrates that past accuracy is as useful in predicting future forecast 
accuracy as the economic determinants identified in the literature mentioned above.  All 
these studies focus on the determinants and persistence of individual analyst accuracy, 
rather than on the market response to individual forecasts. 
 
Our research focus is in identifying analyst attributes that could affect both the 
“reliability” and the “salience” of a forecast revision to market participants.  In this 
context, it is not clear to what extent the market already appreciates the superior forecast 
accuracy of Institutional Investor All-American analysts.  In addition to providing more 
accurate forecasts (Stickel (1992)), these analysts are also selected on the basis of the 
economic insight contained in their written reports, the level of access and the overall 
service they provide to their institutional clients, and the quality of their stock 

--- PAGE 10 ---
 
 
 
9
 
recommendations.  Therefore, these are “celebrity” analysts whose overall reputation 
might affect the way the market participants perceive their revisions. 
 
We therefore use two widely publicized sources to identify award-winning analysts: a.) 
the Institutional Investor’s All-American Research Team, and b.) the Wall Street 
Journal’s Survey of Award Winning Analysts.  The former was used in Stickel (1992), the 
latter is a more recent survey that has, to the best of our knowledge, not been used in 
prior research.  Specifically, we focus on those analysts identified by the Wall Street 
Journal as superior in terms of their ability to forecast earnings accurately (WSJ Earnings 
Estimators).6 
 
The WSJ Earnings Estimators offer an interesting contrast to the II All-Stars.  The 
overlap between the two samples is not large: 23% of the II All-Stars revisions are issued 
by analysts who are also on the WSJ list.  Institutional Investors select their All-Stars 
based on a survey of major buy-side clients, while the WSJ selection process is based 
strictly on forecast accuracy.  Comparing the two lists, the II All-Stars are generally from 
major investment houses, while the WSJ analysts often come from smaller and lesser 
known firms.  II All-Star rankings are also sticky, in that once an analyst acquires All-
Star status, (s)he is likely to continue to be ranked in subsequent years.  The turnover is 
much higher among WSJ rankings, because it is based strictly on accuracy over the past 
12 months.  Therefore, while both types of analysts may have superior forecasting ability, 
the WSJ analysts typically do not enjoy the stature and overall reputation of the II All-
Stars.   
 
If the market pays more attention to “celebrity” analysts, we should expect a stronger 
immediate price response to the forecasts issued by the II All-Stars, followed by a less 
pronounced price drift.  This hypothesis is consistent with experimental evidence from 
the individual decision making literature (e.g., Griffin and Tversky 1992 and Bloomfield 

--- PAGE 11 ---
 
 
 
10
 
et al. 2000).  These studies distinguish information according to two characteristics: 
strength (its salience) and weight (its statistical reliability).  The experimental evidence 
shows that when an investor has imperfect information about signal reliability, the 
salience (or “strength”) of a signal will affect an individual’s perception of its reliability 
(“weight”). 
 
B.3 Firm Attributes 
The overall level of information available about the firm heavily influences the value of 
an individual analyst forecast. Elgers et al. (2001) find that the price response to value-
relevant information in analyst earnings forecasts is less complete for firms with lower 
levels of analyst coverage.7  This is consistent with research by Hong et al. (2000) which 
finds that returns to momentum-based investment strategies are higher for firms with low 
levels of analyst coverage.  If information is impounded in price less efficiently in firms 
with lower levels of analyst coverage then firms with lower levels of analyst coverage 
should have a less complete immediate response to forecast revisions. 
 
In addition to analyst coverage, we include three other firm-level variables to control for 
differences in expected returns arising from the risk profile of the firm.  Specifically, our 
control variables include: firm size (defined as the natural log of a firm’s market 
capitalization measured as of the end of the last calendar year), the book-to-market ratio 
(measured as of the end of the last calendar year), and price momentum (measured as the 
market-adjusted return over the prior six months).  While we do not necessarily believe 
all three variables are proxies for risk, we include them in our analysis because our 
interest is in the incremental ability of analyst forecast revisions to predict subsequent 
returns.

III. SAMPLE SELECTION AND RESEARCH METHODOLOGY 
A. Sample Selection Criteria 
Our data on analyst forecasts are obtained from the I/B/E/S Detail file. Returns data are 
obtained from the Center for Research on Security Prices (CRSP) database.  For each 
firm, we compute size-adjusted returns by subtracting the mean buy-and-hold return of an 
equal-weighted portfolio of firms in the same NYSE size decile over our holding period. 
We use names from the I/B/E/S/ Analyst and Broker Name File to identify individual 
I/B/E/S analysts, and match these names to the “Award Winners” from the Institutional 
Investors (II) and Wall Street Journal (WSJ) publications.  
 
Our sample period begins in October 1993, the first month during which both the 
Institutional Investor magazine and the Wall Street Journal provided a list of ranked 
analysts.  The period ends on December 31, 1998, the last date for which two years of 
returns data are currently available.  Our focus is on forecasts of the one-year-ahead 
annual earnings (FY1).  Our initial sample of 372,021 observations consists of FY1 
forecast revisions with a prior forecast by the same analyst and CRSP return data for the 
firm.  If a stock is delisted during the 2-year return accumulation period, we assume the 
proceeds are re-invested to earn the average return of the matching size decile portfolio.  
Approximately 46% of the revisions in the final sample are for firms traded on the NYSE 
or AMEX.  The remaining 54% are for NASDAQ firms. 
 
B. Signal Attributes 
We first classify individual revisions on the basis of their signal attributes.  Specifically, 
we compare the incoming forecast to the analyst’s own prior forecast, as well as the most 
recent prior consensus forecast.  In our sample, very few forecasts are exactly the same as 
an analyst’s own prior forecast (i.e., analysts rarely initiate a re-statement of an existing 
forecast), and we drop these observations.  
 
We compute the prior consensus forecast by averaging the individual forecasts as of the 
end of the previous day.  Our interest is in mimicking the consensus forecast that is 

--- PAGE 13 ---
 
 
 
12
 
broadly available to users on a daily basis.  Therefore, we include all available individual 
forecasts from active analysts in the IBES database as of any given day.  IBES also 
produces a monthly consensus measure in the third week of every month.  We compared 
our consensus to the IBES consensus and found that our consensus forecast is generally 
quite close to the IBES consensus on the days for which IBES reports a consensus.  
 
Figure 1 illustrates the four possible signal attribute classifications.  The first group 
consists of new forecasts that are lower than both the consensus and the analyst’s own 
prior forecast.  These are deemed “Confirmed Bad Signals” (a “B” signal in Figure 1a).  
The second group consists of new forecasts that are higher than both the consensus and 
the analyst’s own prior forecast.  These are deemed “Confirmed Good Signals” (depicted 
as “G” in Figure 1b).   Two sub-categories of Conflicting signals are also possible.  The 
first group consists of forecasts that are higher than an analyst’s own prior signal but 
lower than the prior consensus (denoted by “C”).  The second group consists of forecasts 
that are lower than an analyst’s own prior signal but higher than the prior consensus 
(denoted by “C'”). In instances where the new forecast is exactly the same as the prior 
consensus, we treat it as a conflicting signal. 
 
In subsequent tests, we investigate the price impact of signals in the confirming versus 
conflicting categories.  Our expectation is that confirming signals will be associated with 
greater price responses during the announcement period.  If the market does make a 
sufficient distinction between analysts that bring new information and those that are 
simply herding toward the consensus, we should also observe a greater subsequent price 
drift for confirming signals.  Because of their ambivalent nature, we expect signals C and 
C' to generate smaller price responses during the announcement period.  We also expect 
these signals to be associated with a smaller subsequent price drift.8

from the consensus) and “converging” signals (those that move toward, and beyond, the consensus).  We

C. Analyst and Firm Attributes 
To identify individual analysts, we obtained the Analyst and Broker Name File from 
IBES.  This file contains the last name and first initial of each analyst, as well as a unique 
analyst code and the code for the brokerage firm.  In those few instances where more than 
one analyst had the same last name and first initial, we used the industry and brokerage 
firm information to distinguish between the analysts.  We then identified the “superior” 
analysts based on the list of names in the award issue of the Institutional Investor or Wall 
Street Journal publications. 
 
Each year, the Institutional Investor magazine ranks individual sell-side analysts, based 
on a survey conducted among institutional investors and buy-side analysts.  The criteria 
used in these rankings are multi-faceted, but according to the magazine they include: the 
insightfulness of the analyst’s overall analysis, his/her accessibility to clients, the 
perceived accuracy of his/her forecasts, and other considerations.  The Institutional 
Investor publishes its rankings annually in October. We consider an analyst an “All-Star” 
if he/she appears in the II All-American list in the previous year.  Analysts ranked first, 
second, third or runner-up in the annual Institutional Investor list are classified as an All-
Star for the 12 months following their inclusion in the survey. 
  
Next, we consider a new category of analysts based on the Wall Street Journal (WSJ) 
survey.  The WSJ began publishing its own independent ranking of sell-side analysts in 
September 1993.  In subsequent years, this ranking has been published in June, beginning 
with June of 1994. The WSJ survey distinguishes between analysts who excel at 
forecasting annual earnings, and analysts who excel in terms of their ability to issue stock 
recommendations.  We focus on the top earnings estimators.  Specifically, analysts are 
classified as superior earnings forecasters (“Earnings Estimators”) for the 12 months 
following their inclusion in the Wall Street Journal rankings in first through fifth place.9

Finally, we also examine the effect of firm specific characteristics on the price discovery 
process.  Following Elgers et al. (2001), we classify firms based on whether they are 
followed by an above or below median number of analysts.  We measure the number of 
analyst covering a firm as the maximum number of analysts with outstanding forecasts at 
any point during the year.  High (low) analyst coverage firms are those above (below) the 
median for our sample in a given year.

B. Multivariate Analysis 
The evidence thus far provides some support for the view that the market adjustment is 
less complete for confirming signals.  One interpretation of this finding is that the market 
does not fully distinguish between analysts with new information and those that are 
simply herding toward the consensus.  Another possibility is that confirming signals are 
simply larger revisions, and the greater market under-reaction is a function of the 
magnitude of the revision.  Moreover, although we use size-adjusted returns, we have not 
controlled for other differences in risk-related characteristics across firms in the long and 
short portfolios.  It is possible that confirming signals are simply associated with firms 
that are riskier, and the higher returns we observe are a compensation for that risk.   
 
To address these issues, we conduct a multivariate analysis that includes a number of 
firm-level control variables, as well as the magnitude of the revision itself.  The firm 
financial data needed for this analysis are obtained from the Compustat Price, Dividend 
and Earnings file. Use of this data reduced the sample size to 276,134 observations in the 
most restrictive multivariate tests.

The variables in the multivariate analysis include: market-adjusted returns over the prior 
six months (Momentum); the natural log of a firm’s market capitalization at the end of 
the last calendar year (Size); and a firm’s book-to-market ratio as of the end of the last 
calendar year (B/M).  All three variables have been identified by prior studies as 
predictors of cross-sectional returns.12   
 
We also include an indicator variable associated with the amount of analyst coverage a 
firm receives (Analyst Coverage).  This variable is assigned a value of 1 if a firm is 
followed by more than the median number of analysts and 0 otherwise.  The number of 
analysts for this analysis is determined annually, based on the maximum number making 
a one-year-ahead forecast for a given firm in a given year.    
 
Finally, we include two measures associated with the revision signal itself.  Revise is a 
measure of the magnitude of the forecast revision.  To compute this variable, we subtract 
an analyst’s own prior forecast from the new forecast, and express the difference as a 
percentage of the closing price on day t-2.  We also create a categorical variable that 
captures the incremental effect of classifying a revision as a confirming rather than 
conflicting signal:  Signal = +1 for confirming good news, =0 for conflicting news, and 
=-1 for confirming bad news.  To limit the effect of outliers, the extreme top and bottom 
one percent of the B/M and Size and Revise variables are winsorized.

To assess the effect of analyst reputation, we group analysts into three categories: Non-

by II-All Stars.  The WSJ rankings group firms into fewer industries, identifying just over 
10% of the analysts as Award Winners.  The overlap in award winners between the two 
publications is not large – 23% of the II All-Stars are also WSJ Earnings-Estimators.

surrounding the release of their forecast revisions. 
 
The evidence on the post-revision price drift is less clear.  Compared to the revisions by

ranked analysts are mixed.  In most instances it appears that WSJ analysts are associated

significant.  One limitation of this test is that it only controls for signal attributes, not

examine this issue in a multiple-regression framework.

The result on analyst identity is also interesting.  All-Star x Signal is reliably negative

when the analyst is an II All-Star.  However, Earnings Estimator x Signal is reliably

The evidence shows that, once we have controlled for those analysts that appear in both 
rankings, the WSJ Award Winners are associated with a greater price drifts.   One 
interpretation of this result is that WSJ analysts (who are selected strictly on the basis of 
their superior ability to predict earnings), is bringing more valuable information to 
market.  The higher short-window response associated with these analysts show the 
market recognizes this fact to some degree.  However, the greater price drift implies that 
a larger proportion of the information associated with the WSJ analysts is impounded into 
price with a delay.  This result is consistent with the fact that revisions by WSJ Award 
Winners (who tend to be from smaller, lesser known, investment firms) are less “salient” 
to investors than revisions by II All-Stars.    
 
To better understand the nature of the delayed price response, we conduct a further test 
involving subsequent earnings event dates.  Specifically, we examine the abnormal return 
observed in three-day windows centered on subsequent earnings announcements and 
forecast revisions.  If the post-revision price drift is due to a delayed market response to 
current information about future earnings, and this misperception is corrected when 
further information about future earnings are released, then we might expect subsequent 
abnormal returns to be clustered around the future dates associated with the release of 
earnings news.  Conversely, if the price drift is due to a mis-specified risk model, we 
should not observe higher abnormal returns in the narrow-event windows around the 
release of subsequent earnings news.

market is adjusting to analyst forecast revisions slowly, and that subsequent forecast 
revisions by other analysts serve as catalysts in this sluggish price formation process. 
 
V. CONCLUSION 
 
Much of capital market research in accounting over the past 20 years has assumed that 
the price adjustment process to information is instantaneous and/or trivial.  This basic 
assumption has had an enormous influence on the way we select research topics, design 
empirical tests, and interpret research findings.  In this study, we provide evidence that 
the price discovery process, at least as it pertains to analyst forecast revisions, is a 
complex process that deserves more attention. 
 
Using a large sample of individual forecast revisions, we conjecture, and show, that the 
magnitude of the delayed price reaction is a function of various signal, firm, and analyst 
characteristics.  Specifically, we show that the market does not seem to make a sufficient 
distinction between analysts who bring new information to market and those that are 
simply “herding” toward the consensus forecast.  This result holds after controlling for 
firm size, B/M, price momentum, and the size of the forecast revision itself. 
 

--- PAGE 26 ---
 
 
 
25
 
We also show that the salience of a signal, as measured by an analyst’s reputation, can 
affect the speed and efficacy of the price response.  The immediate market reaction is 
stronger for both II All-Stars and WSJ Award Winners.  A greater proportion of the total 
price reaction for these “celebrity” analysts occurs in the 3-day window surrounding the 
news release.  In addition, the delayed price reaction is reliably weaker for II All-Stars.  
In contrast, the delayed price reaction is reliably stronger for WSJ Award Winners, who 
are picked on the basis of their past accuracy and who are more likely to be from smaller 
firms. 
 
Finally, we show that the delayed price response to forecast revisions is reliably lower 
when firms are covered by a greater number of analysts.  This effect is most pronounced 
in the first six months, and dissipates over the next 24 months, suggesting that analyst 
coverage affects the speed of the price adjustment process rather than the overall 
magnitude. 
 
We also find that a substantial portion of the delayed price reaction occurs around 
subsequent earnings announcements.  An even greater portion of the delayed reaction is 
observed around future forecast revisions.  Indeed, we find that the short-window returns 
around the next six forecast revisions are at least partially predictable on the basis of the 
current forecast revision.  This evidence suggests that the market is adjusting slowly to 
the information contained in individual forecast revisions, and that later revisions by 
other analysts act as catalysts in the price formation process. 
 
In sum, the market seems to miss certain qualitative aspects of earnings revisions.  After 
controlling for the sign and magnitude of the forecast revision itself, as well as various 
risk-based factors, it appears that the market still under-appreciates more subtle aspects of 
the revision.  On the other hand, the market seems capable of a faster and more complete 
assimilation of the earnings information when a.) there are more analysts following the 
firm, and b.) the analyst issuing the forecast is an II All-Star.  These findings help us to 

--- PAGE 27 ---
 
 
 
26
 
better understand the price discovery mechanism, and the boundaries of market 
efficiency. 
 
Another interpretation of our results is that “good news” firms (i.e. firms whose earnings 
have just been revised upwards) are somehow riskier than “bad news” firms. Cast in this 
light, the higher average returns earned by good news firms represent compensation for 
this higher level or risk. This interpretation allows us to remain within the comfortable 
confines of the efficient market hypothesis. However, it appears inconsistent with several 
aspects of our evidence. 
 
First, it leaves open questions as to why firm-specific risk would be positively correlated 
with the release of good news about earnings.13  Second, it begs the question what other 
risk factor might be missing after we have controlled for firm size, B/M, and price 
momentum.  Finally, it does not explain why a large portion of the abnormal returns 
occurs around the dates of subsequent earnings news releases.  Given this evidence, we 
are skeptical of the risk-based explanation.   
 
Finally, our findings are related to the current debate over Regulation FD.  Under this 
regulation, which came into effect in October 2001, management is prohibited from 
discussing company prospects with an analyst unless the same information is 
simultaneously conveyed to others, including other analysts.  Our evidence suggests that 
individual financial analysts bring relevant information to market, and that multiple 
analysts enhance the speed of the price discovery process.  One cannot help but wonder 
whether this would continue to hold under the new regulations.  At first blush, it does 
seem unlikely that the forecast revisions of individual analysts would become more 
useful under Regulation FD.  This appears to be an interesting venue for future research.
