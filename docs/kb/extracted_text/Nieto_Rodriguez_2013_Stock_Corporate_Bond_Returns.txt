Correlation between Individual Stock and Corporate Bond Returns 
Belén Nieto 
Rosa Rodríguez 
 
December 2013 
 
 
ABSTRACT 
 
This paper studies the correlations between individual bond and stock returns issued by the 
same firm using Trading Reporting and Compliance Engine prices. We employ panel data 
to analyze the determinants of the variation in estimated dynamic correlations using 
macroeconomic cycle indicators, firm risk measures, and specific bond characteristics. The 
results show that the correlations vary both over time and cross-sectionally. Specifically, 
aggregate factors approximating economic growth are not related to changes in 
correlations, but correlations decrease with negative expectations about future aggregate 
risks, although only for firms with a low default probability. However, the correlations are 
higher when the stock idiosyncratic risk and/or firm financial leverage increases. In 
contrast, the relation between correlations and the systematic stock risk is negative. 
 
JEL Classification: G12, G14 
Keywords: individual corporate bonds, stock–bond return correlation, idiosyncratic risk, 
economic cycles.

1. Introduction 
The correlation between bonds and stocks has been widely investigated, but only at the 
aggregate level. In other words, most of the previous research consists of analyzing the 
comovements in the two markets as a whole, and mostly regarding government bonds. The 
results from these studies do not provide consistent conclusions; they depend on the 
methodology employed, the sample period, and/or the country. Thus, papers using data 
before the 1990s find a small but positive long-term correlation between stocks and 
Treasury bonds (e.g., Shiller and Beltratti, 1992; Campbell and Ammer, 1993).  The equity 
and bond markets have behaved very differently since the late 1990s, displaying mostly a 
negative correlation between themselves.1 The literature offers theoretical explanations for 
both signs.2 In any case, there is no doubt that this correlation is time varying. Gulko 
(2002), Illmanen (2003), Connolly, Stivers, and Sun (2005), and Baele, Bekaert, and 
Inghelbrecht (2010) document that the stock–Treasury bond correlation exhibits positive 
values in stable periods and decreases significantly in subsequent crisis periods. This 
finding is justified by the “flight to safety” effect, where investors’ risk aversion increases 
during recessions, shifting investments from stocks to bonds.3 That is, contrary to what 
occurs in expansions, bonds are viewed as hedge instruments in recessions. In any case, the 
question of what drives the stock–bond correlation remains open, as shown by the profuse 
amount of research employing different methodologies surrounding this issue.4

Research papers regarding the comovements between stocks and corporate bonds 
are scarce and consist mainly of analyzing whether a variable related to one security is 
relevant in determining a characteristic about the other security. An accepted conclusion is 
that equity volatility contains relevant information for explaining or predicting credit 
spreads, as shown by Campbell and Taskler (2003), Cremers, Driessen, Maenhout, and 
Weinbaum (2008), and Zhang, Zhou, and Zhu (2009). 
Our paper contributes by studying the correlation between individual stock and 
corporate bond returns to identify what variables explain correlation variability. The goal of 
understanding whether the two main financing securities of a firm are correlated or not and 
why has important implications for both corporate finance and portfolio investment 
decisions. From the corporate finance perspective, evidence about the reactions of stock 
and bond prices to either idiosyncratic or systematic news is certainly relevant for dynamic 
capital structure decisions over the economic cycle. A key issue of capital structure is to 
understand how and why capital structure choice varies with macroeconomic conditions 
and with firm-specific characteristics. In addition, the market value of the leverage ratio is 
affected by the correlation between the numerator and the denominator. Therefore, 
analyzing how this correlation changes both throughout the economic cycle and with the 
firm’s particular idiosyncratic situation would help in choosing dynamically optimal 
leverage ratios. From the portfolio management perspective, this study may also contribute 
by identifying new diversification possibilities. In the case of negative or very low 
correlations, the combination of bonds and stocks issued by the same firm should reduce 
the investment portfolio’s total risk. 
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first paper with this aim. Schaefer and 
Strebulaev (2008) analyze the sensitivity of corporate bond returns to changes in equity 
returns (also referred to as the hedge ratio) under the structural model of Merton (1974). 
They show that the estimated hedge ratios are positive and statistically significant and 
increase with firm risk. This finding is consistent with one of our empirical results. 
However, their paper does not consider either dynamic correlations or covariances and their 
analysis is reduced to variables within the selected model. On the other hand, Kwan (1996) 
studies the correlation between individual stock returns and changes in bond yields 
calculated from the bid prices of bonds quoted by Bloomberg. Kwan finds a negative

correlation that could be driven by firm-specific information more related to the mean than 
to the variance of the firm’s underlying assets. In contrast, our paper directly uses bond 
returns computed from transaction prices available in the Trading Reporting and 
Compliance Engine (TRACE) database, to which all members must report over-the-counter

affected by selection bias, employs effectively informative prices, and compares the 
movements of two homogeneously computed variables: the returns on the two securities. 
We first estimate dynamic correlations between a set of individual stock and bond 
returns using three alternative methodologies: a rolling sample correlation, a dynamic 
conditional correlation (DCC) model such as Engle’s (2002), and a corrected version of the 
DCC model that incorporates a non-synchronous trading adjustment that follows the initial 
proposal of Engle, Kane, and Noh (1996). Second, we investigate the sources of the 
variation behind the correlations dynamics in both the cross-sectional and time series 
dimensions, using panel data analysis. We consider a large set of potentially explanatory 
variables that include macroeconomic and financial aggregate factors related to economic 
cycles, variables representing both the operational and financial risks of the issuer, and 
variables representing bond characteristics or proxies for the specific risk of bondholders. 
Our results suggest that correlations between individual bond and stock returns are, 
on average, small but very different, depending on the stock–bond pair, and they are also 
clearly time varying, showing persistence. The analysis of the potential determinants 
reveals that the correlations do not respond to cycle indicators related to macroeconomic 
growth but diminish when negative expectations are measured with aggregate risk 
indicators, such as the volatility of consumption growth or the default spread. We also find 
that the correlation diminishes as the systematic stock risk (beta) increases. Hence, bonds 
and stocks of the same firm become increasingly different the greater the uncertainty in the 
overall economy and the higher the stock’s systematic risk. This finding suggests that it 
would be possible to obtain diversification benefits by combining stock and bonds issued 
by the same firm in the investment portfolio at the right moment in time; total portfolio risk 
will be especially lower in bad economic moments and for firms with stocks highly 
sensitive to changes in the stock market. Indeed, this is consistent with the evidence 
reported by Levy and Hennessy (2007), who show that relatively less constrained firms

outstanding equity. In contrast, the correlation increases with the firm’s specific risk. 
Therefore, the final diversification effect will then depend on the relative importance of

The finding of a positive relation between the bond–stock correlation and the firm’s 
specific risk supports the foundation of structural models in which both financial securities 
represent the same productive assets and then both react in the same direction to common 
shocks. Especially relevant is the role of the leverage ratio in explaining correlations, 
particularly for firms with a high probability of default. This result could be useful for 
capital structure decisions because a corporate decision regarding debt (equity) will affect 
the market leverage ratio not only through the numerator (denominator), but also through 
the market value of equity (debt). Then, the speed of adjustment toward target leverage 
should be greater for firms and moments with positive correlation. Consistently, Byoun 
(2008) finds that the speed of adjustment is higher for firms with market debt ratios above 
their target, and lower for firms with market debt ratios below their target level and binding 
financial constraints. 
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 theoretically justifies 
the existence of a non-zero correlation between bond and stock returns and summarizes 
related empirical findings. Section 3 describes the data. Section 4 explains the 
methodologies and the results for the dynamic estimation of the correlations. In Section 5, 
we analyze the determinants of bond–stock correlations, using panel data techniques. 
Section 6 presents a series of robustness checks. We conclude the paper in Section 7.

be negative if the opposite effect that the risk-neutral probability of default plays in the 
price of the two assets is predominant. 
The aim of our paper is to analyze how the correlation between stock and bond 
moves and what variables are responsible for such variations in time and across different 
firms and/or specific bond characteristics. It must be noted that our study does not test any 
particular model for pricing risky debt. The previous discussion about the implications of 
some models on the correlation is just to generate predictions in terms of theoretical 
support. Next, the empirical results will either confirm these predictions or not. 
3. Data 
We employ the transaction database TRACE that compiles information about all corporate 
bond transactions. It is a system by which all members must report any OTC corporate 
transaction in the secondary market following the rules approved by the Security and 
Exchange Commission in January 2001. The actual reporting started in July 2002, with the 
dissemination of all trades in bonds with an initial issuance above $1 billion and in the 50 
highest-yield bonds. In March 2003 the dissemination was extended to trades in bonds with 
an initial issuance above $100 million and a rating of at least A-. Finally, the dissemination

instead of assuming a determined model for the correlations, we estimate the correlations 
with purely data-driven methods in the next section. After that, we attempt to explain their 
cross-sectional and time series variations with variables that can potentially do so.

extensively studied in the literature. The most employed estimation methods are versions of 
multivariate generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH) models.14

being modeled is high. The DCC model of Engle (2002) deals with this problem by 
employing the decomposition of the covariance into volatilities and correlations. Then, the

The estimation requires the selection of instruments, which implies deciding how 
many instruments to use and controlling for the validity of the selection. Natural candidate 
instruments are the first lag (and the first differenced lag) of dependent variables but it is 
possible to employ more lags and to also include the explanatory variables or their lags as 
additional instruments. Using longer lags and/or more instrumental variables introduces 
more information and thus improves the efficiency of the estimation. However, numerous 
papers (Sargan, 1958; Bowsher, 2002; Roodman, 2009b) show that too many instruments 
can induce estimation problems, such as difficulty calculating the inverse of the estimated 
variance matrix, potential bias in the two-step estimated standard errors, or weakness in the 
test for overidentifying restrictions (Hansen, 1982). The first problem can be resolved by 
performing a generalized inverse. The bias in the standard errors can be controlled by using 
the correction proposed by Windmeijer (2005), which produces robust GMM estimators, as 
shown in simulations. However, the potential weakness in the Hansen test has no reliable 
solution. Therefore, there is a tradeoff between whether or not to increase the number of 
instruments. After several empirical estimations comparing the performance of the models, 
we decide to employ lags of the dependent variable to instrument the system GMM and to 
consider all explanatory variables as strictly exogenous. Regarding the number of lags, we 
adopt two alternatives. To be parsimonious, the first includes the minimum number of lags 
such that the set of instruments is not potentially invalid. We use the autocorrelation test of 
Arellano and Bond and the Hansen test, defined below, to determine the validity of the 
instruments. To control for the lack of information when too few lags are employed, the 
second alternative is to estimate the model by collapsing the instrument set following 
Roodman (2009b). Collapsing the instrument set allows all possible lags to be employed 
but reduces the standard number of moment conditions by linear combinations. This 
method conveys slightly less information than the standard one while embodying the same 
expectation in the moment condition set. In both options, we compute standard errors 
applying the Windmeijer correction for robust estimators. 
Regarding the validity of the chosen instruments, two tests can be used. The first, 
due to Arellano and Bond (1991), tests the null of the absence of autocorrelation in the 
idiosyncratic error term of the model. The test is applied to the residuals in differences. By 
definition, these residuals present first-order serial correlation. In fact, this indicates that the

assumption of a dynamic model is correct. Thus, the first-order serial correlation in levels is

correlation is rejected, then the second lag of the dependent variable is potentially an 
invalid instrument and three or more lags would be needed. 
Despite the persistence in the residuals, a set of valid instruments must also be 
assumed to be exogenous to conclude the validity of the system GMM. In an attempt to 
verify this assumption, the Sargan (1958) or Hansen (1982) test can be applied. In both 
cases, the null is that the set of sample moment conditions holds, which is tested by a 
quadratic form statistic that is asymptotically chi-square distributed and the unique 
difference is the weighting matrix. The Hansen test proposes an optimal weighting matrix 
that can be estimated in a two-step procedure. The problem with this test is that it is 
weakened by instrument proliferation. In contrast, the Sargan test is not weakened because 
of a high number of instruments but the weighting matrix that it employs is a consistent 
estimator of the covariance matrix of the errors only under homoskedasticity. Otherwise, 
the Sargan test tends to over-reject the null. Therefore, a theoretically superior 
overidentifying restrictions test is based on the Hansen statistic from a two-step estimate. In 
any case, we provide both tests for all our estimated models.

6.2. Interactions 
The previous results show inconsistencies in the relation between stock–bond correlations 
and some explanatory variables. For example, the parameter of VIX is significant, 
depending on the model, and the relation between correlations and firm default probability 
appears irrelevant in the panel estimation case but relevant when the relation is estimated 
by the Fama–MacBeth method. It is possible that these inconsistencies are related to 
potential interactions between the economic cycle and the risk position of individual firms. 
In this section we analyze this possibility with the theoretical support of a model that 
combines the time series and cross-sectional dimensions of the problem: the intertemporal 
structural model proposed by Bhamra Kuehn and Strebulaev (2010). 
Under this model, the sources of risk for both  the firm’s stocks and bonds are 
aggregate consumption and the individual firm’s earnings. On the one hand, negative 
changes in the next period’s consumption growth or a negative revision in expectations 
about consumption growth in the future increases the price of risk, which would have 
negative consequences for both the stock and the bond. On the other hand, firm earnings 
volatility is responsible of the price of the default claim; while an increase in firm risk 
would have a negative effect on bond price, its effect on the stock price is a priori

ambiguous because it is also affected by the call structure of the equity value. Moreover, 
these two sources of risk are positively correlated.24 
With this framework in mind, we would expect interactions between state or 
aggregate variables and variables related to firm risk. To analyze if this is the case, we split 
the sample of firms into two subsamples on the base of the firm default probability. 
Specifically, each month in our sample period we classify a firm as having a high 
probability of default if its probability of default is higher than the 75th percentile for the 
cross-sectional distribution of that month. Otherwise, the firm is in the low (normal) default 
probability subsample. We adopt this method of splitting the sample because it produces 
the highest differences in the mean level of default probability between the subsamples. In 
addition, this division produces the most symmetric distribution of firms: the median 
number of firms in each subsample is exactly the same.25 After that, the number of stock–
bond correlations in each subsample depends on the number of bonds belonging to each 
firm and, of course, on the specific month. On average, over time, the number of 
correlations is 165 in the high default probability subsample and 300 in the low default 
probability subsample, with a maximum of 239 firms in the former subsample in November 
2008. Then, we repeat the panel model estimations for each subsample separately.26 The 
estimated results are reported in Tables 7 and 8 for the low and high default probability 
subsamples, respectively.

7. Summary and conclusions 
The correlation between bond and stock returns at the aggregate market level is a widely 
investigated topic. However, papers seeking to explain the commonality between the bond 
and stock returns of assets issued by the same firm are scarce, maybe because of the lack of 
a continuous and reliable database for corporate bond transaction prices. We attempt to 
cover this gap by estimating and analyzing the dynamics of the correlations between 
individual stock and bond returns. We employ corporate bond transaction prices from 
TRACE, a system by which all members must report OTC corporate transactions in the 
secondary market as of July 2002. 
The first part of the paper estimates the correlation series for each stock–bond pair 
in our sample, using different approaches: a rolling window sample correlation, a DCC 
model, and a variation of the previous model to incorporate the non-synchronous trading 
problem. Our results indicate that the correlations between individual bond and stock 
returns are small, on average, but definitively time varying. The dynamics in the 
correlations are reasonably well captured by models showing persistence, and non-trading

The aim of the second part of the paper is to identify the sources of the variability of 
these correlations over time and across firms and/or bond issues. For this purpose we 
consider a large set of potential explanatory variables, including economic cycle indicators, 
different measures of issuer risk, and also characteristics regarding the specific bond 
contract. We employ both panel data analysis and the Fama–MacBeth cross-sectional 
estimation methodology. 
We find interesting and quite robust results regarding the three sets of estimated 
correlations, the two alternative estimation methods, and for different model specifications 
and/or instrumentations. Variables approximating macroeconomic growth, such as IPI 
growth, aggregate consumption growth, the term structure spread, or the dummy NBER 
cycle indicator, are not related to the correlations. Only variables representing aggregate 
risk can explain the correlation variability. Specifically, increases in the volatility of 
consumption growth or in the aggregate default premium are related to decreases in the 
correlations, but only for normal levels of firm risk measured by the firm probability of 
default. Therefore, it seems that aggregate sources of risk affect individual corporate bond 
and stock prices in opposite directions (or, alternatively, affect stock prices but not bond 
prices), although their effect is only present when the firm probability of default is not 
highly extreme. On the one hand, some measures of firm risk are significantly related to 
changes in the correlations. The effect of these firm variables changes depending on the 
type of risk. While the correlation increases with the idiosyncratic risk or risk not 
associated to aggregate risk factors, it decreases when the systematic risk and, specifically, 
operational systematic risk increases. That is, variables such as the leverage ratio or the 
non-systematic component of stock volatility are positively related to correlations. This, 
therefore, indicates the same direction of the reactions of both stock and bond prices to 
increases in this type of risk, consistent with the structural model prediction of a positive 
correlation since both financial assets represent the same productive assets. However, an 
increase in the systematic component of firm stock return variability, measured as the 
unlevered market beta, is associated with a reduction in the correlation between the returns 
of stocks and bonds, probably because it negatively affects stock prices but has no effect on

Finally, in general terms, neither variables related to liquidity regarding any of the two 
assets nor the characteristics of the bond issue, such as bondholder protection or the coupon 
level (or the maturity when the firm’s risk is high), determine changes in the correlations. 
An interesting and surprising result from our analysis is that the information content 
in the expectation about stock market risk (VIX) is closely related to the average of the 
non-systematic component of firm risk, especially when firms have a high specific risk. 
Then, we also find that VIX explains correlations with a positive sign precisely for these 
highly risky firms. 
Our results have important practical implications. On the one hand, for the 
investment portfolio management framework, they suggest new diversification 
possibilities. We find that the correlation between the bond and stock returns issued by the 
same firm decreases as some measures of risk increase. For example, the combination of 
the two assets in the same portfolio can reduce the global risk in moments of high stock 
systematic risk or before increases in the aggregate consumption risk and/or aggregate 
default risk in the case of firms with standard levels of default probability. In contrast, the 
benefits of such diversification are reduced when the firm has high levels of idiosyncratic 
risk, especially if the firm is highly leveraged. On the other hand, our results can also be 
useful from a corporate finance perspective. They suggest that some corporate decisions 
that increment a firm’s specific risk, such as an increase in the leverage ratio, can reduce 
firm value not only via equity value but also via debt value. Therefore, the identification of 
the moments when a firm stock–bond correlation is positive and the level of this correlation 
can help firm managers in choosing dynamically optimal leverage ratios.
