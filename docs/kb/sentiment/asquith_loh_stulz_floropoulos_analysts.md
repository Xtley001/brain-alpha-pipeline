## 🔬 Section I: Comprehensive Scientific Overview

### 1. Asquith, Mikhail & Au (2005): Information Content & Price Target Dominance
- **Price Target Revision Superiority:** Changes in analyst price targets ($\Delta Target / Price$) have a larger, more statistically significant market reaction than discrete recommendation changes or earnings forecast revisions. Incorporating target price revisions dramatically increases regression explanatory power.
- **Subsuming of Earnings Forecasts:** When price targets, recommendation changes, and report text arguments are evaluated simultaneously, earnings forecast revisions are partially subsumed by price targets.
- **Asymmetry in Revisions:** Market reaction is heavily asymmetric. Downgrades receive the most scrutiny and generate large, persistent negative abnormal returns. Upgrades are discounted by institutional investors because 55% of all recommendations are buys/strong buys and only 6% are sells (structural sell-side optimism bias).
- **Target Accuracy Realities:** Only ~54% of analysts' price targets are met or exceeded within 12 months. Extreme analyst optimism (implied return $> 50\%$) is inversely related to the probability of achieving the target.

### 2. Loh & Stulz (2018): Sell-Side Research Value in Bad Times
- **Surge in Analyst Impact During Crises:** In bad times (NBER recessions, credit crises, high VIX, policy uncertainty), the 2-day abnormal stock price impact of recommendation and earnings forecast revisions is substantially greater than in good times for both upgrades and downgrades.
- **The Bayesian Uncertainty Mechanism:** In bad times, macro uncertainty surges and private investor signal precision plunges. In a Bayesian updating framework, rational investors place higher weight on sell-side analyst signals:
  $$w_{analyst} = \frac{\tau_{analyst}}{\tau_{prior} + \tau_{analyst}}$$
- **Forecast Error Per Unit of Uncertainty:** Although raw absolute forecast errors rise during crises, forecast error *per unit of prior volatility* actually decreases. Analysts work harder, issue longer reports, and provide more frequent updates during bad times.
- **Firm Complexity Interaction:** The increase in sell-side analyst impact during bad times is strongest for harder-to-value, complex firms with high intangible assets and high idiosyncratic uncertainty.

### 3. Floropoulos (2018): Short-Term vs. Long-Term Forecast Horizons
- **The Two-Year (FY2) Informativeness Peak:** Reports containing 2-year ahead annual forecasts (FY2) are the most informative in financial markets (average abnormal reaction 0.42%), significantly higher than reports with only 1-year ahead forecasts (0.30%).
- **Concordant vs. Contrasting Forecast Revisions:**
  - When FY1 and FY2 forecasts are revised in the *same* direction (concordant revision), the market reaction is almost double (0.60%+).
  - When FY1 is revised up but FY2 is revised down (contrasting revisions), the market response follows the longer-term FY2 signal, treating FY1 as temporary transient noise.
- **Three-Year (FY3) Disclosure Bias:** Disclosure of 3-year forecasts (FY3) occurs selectively when firm performance is ambiguous, exhibiting asymmetric optimistic bias.

## 📖 Section II: Exact Academic Extracts ($\ge 20$ Verbatim Quotes)

### Extract 1: Asquith, Mikhail & Au (2005) — Abstract & Market Reaction
> "This paper investigates the market reaction to the information released in security analyst reports. It shows that the market reacts significantly and positively to changes in recommendation levels, earnings forecasts, and price targets. While changes in price targets and earnings forecasts both provide information to the market, revisions in price targets have a larger and more significant impact than comparable revisions in earnings forecasts." (p. 2)

### Extract 2: Asquith, Mikhail & Au (2005) — Subsuming of Earnings Forecasts
> "The text of the report is also a significant source of information as it provides the justifications supporting an analyst’s summary opinion. When all of this information is considered simultaneously, some of it, notably the earnings forecasts, is subsumed." (p. 2)

### Extract 3: Asquith, Mikhail & Au (2005) — Target Accuracy Benchmark
> "The results further show that analysts correctly predict price targets slightly over 50% of the time (54%). Finally, the valuation methodology used does not seem to be correlated with either the market’s reaction or the analyst’s accuracy." (p. 2)

### Extract 4: Asquith, Mikhail & Au (2005) — Structural Sell-Side Optimism Bias
> "Mikhail, Walther, and Willis (2002) report that approximately 55% of all recommendations are buys or strong buys, 39% are holds and only 6% are sells or strong sells. An often-cited rationale for this avoidance is that an analyst’s salary and bonus may be linked to quantifiable measures such as his firm’s underwriting fees or commissions generated by his recommendations." (p. 3)

### Extract 5: Asquith, Mikhail & Au (2005) — Fit Improvement with Price Targets
> "Our analysis shows that changes in the summary stock recommendations, earnings forecasts, and price targets all provide independent information to the capital markets. In particular, incorporating changes in analyst price targets dramatically increases the fit of our regression results over that obtained from earnings forecast revisions and discrete recommendations alone." (p. 4)

### Extract 6: Asquith, Mikhail & Au (2005) — Downgrade Scrutiny Asymmetry
> "We find that the contents of an analyst’s report receive the most scrutiny in the case of downgrades. The changes in a firm’s price target, earnings forecasts and the strength of a report’s arguments are all positively correlated with the market’s response... Moreover, while recommendation downgrades remain statistically negative, the significance of recommendation upgrades is eliminated." (p. 4–5)

### Extract 7: Asquith, Mikhail & Au (2005) — Brokerage Affiliation Conflict
> "The coefficient on our proxy for a relationship between a brokerage and a firm under analysis is statistically negative. This suggests that the market discounts good news and amplifies bad news when the brokerage is not independent of the firm." (p. 5)

### Extract 8: Asquith, Mikhail & Au (2005) — Target Optimism Inversely Predicts Success
> "Approximately 54% of analysts’ price targets are achieved or exceeded. The remaining 46% of firms achieve an average of 84% of the price target within 12 months. The level of optimism exhibited by an analyst, as measured by the projected change in a firm’s stock price, appears to be inversely related to the probability of achieving a particular target." (p. 5)

### Extract 9: Loh & Stulz (2018) — Abstract & Bad Times Impact
> "Because uncertainty is high in bad times, investors find it harder to assess firm prospects and, hence, should value analyst output more... We find that, in bad times, analyst revisions have a larger stock-price impact, earnings forecast errors per unit of uncertainty fall, reports are more frequent and longer, and the impact of analyst output increases more for harder-to-value firms." (p. 2)

### Extract 10: Loh & Stulz (2018) — Core Empirical Finding on Two-Day Returns
> "Using the average two-day abnormal returns to stock recommendation changes, we find that analysts are more impactful during bad times for both downgrades and upgrades. Further, using the definition of influential recommendations in Loh and Stulz (2011), which effectively treats recommendation changes as influential if the stock-price reaction is statistically significant, we find robust evidence that both upgrades and downgrades are more likely to be influential during bad times compared to good times." (p. 4)

### Extract 11: Loh & Stulz (2018) — Market Uncertainty vs Firm-Level Uncertainty
> "We decompose a firm’s total stock return volatility into market, industry, and firm-specific components. We find that the increased impact of recommendation changes in times of high uncertainty is most robust when the market component is used to define high uncertainty." (p. 4)

### Extract 12: Loh & Stulz (2018) — Robustness Against Other Corporate News Events
> "Adapting the methodology in Frankel et al. (2006), we regress a stock’s daily absolute returns on a comprehensive set of dummy variables... Interacting these news dummies with bad times indicators, we show that not all firm news events are associated with greater bad times impact. Importantly, the market reacts more to recommendation changes in bad times even after all other news events and their interactions with bad times are controlled for." (p. 4–5)

### Extract 13: Loh & Stulz (2018) — Bayesian Precision per Unit of Uncertainty
> "Consider investors who receive a new signal from analysts. The extent to which that signal will change their priors depends on the weight that investors put on the new signal and on the weight they put on their prior (Pastor and Veronesi 2009)... In bad times, investors will put more weight on a signal from an analyst if the ratio of the precision of the signal to the uncertainty of the prior increases... Using prior volatility to normalize absolute forecast errors, we find that this adjusted forecast precision actually increases during bad times." (p. 5)

### Extract 14: Loh & Stulz (2018) — Harder-to-Value Firms Experience Largest Surge
> "The impact of analyst output increases more for harder-to-value firms in bad times. Specifically, firms with higher idiosyncratic volatility, smaller market capitalization, and fewer institutional investors experience the greatest increase in the price impact of sell-side research during market downturns." (p. 18)

### Extract 15: Floropoulos (2018) — Two-Year Ahead Forecast Dominance
> "Results suggest that investors take into account all available information in analyst reports. Main findings also show that when the analysts provide forecasts for at least two annual periods, the market response is similar for comparable information regardless of the amount of forecasts included in the report, indicating that investors extrapolate information for longer-term periods from the estimates provided." (p. 1)

### Extract 16: Floropoulos (2018) — One-Year Forecast Insufficiency
> "On the contrary, when reports include only one annual earnings prediction the market response is significantly smaller. Analysts are more likely to disclose forecasts for more periods when the firms’ future performance is not clearly positive or clearly negative to provide more detailed information." (p. 1)

### Extract 17: Floropoulos (2018) — Quantitative Informativeness Spectrum
> "I find that the reports that include long-term forecasts are more informative than those disclosing only one-year-ahead predictions with the two-year-horizon reports being the most informative. In the full sample, the informativeness of two-year-horizon reports is 0.42% followed by that of reports with more long-term estimates (0.35%-0.36%), while one-year-horizon reports are the least informative (0.30%)." (p. 4)

### Extract 18: Floropoulos (2018) — Concordant Revisions Double Abnormal Returns
> "Similar to the results for the full sample, when all the forecasts that are included in a report are revised in the same direction, reports with horizons of three or more years are more informative than those comprising only one-year-ahead (FY1) forecasts, but less than two-year-horizon reports. In this case, the abnormal returns of reports with two-year horizons are almost the double than those with predictions for only one years’ earnings." (p. 4)

### Extract 19: Floropoulos (2018) — Contrasting Revisions Resolution
> "When reports have contrasting revisions between FY1 and FY2, investors prioritize the longer-term FY2 prediction. A positive FY1 revision accompanied by a negative FY2 revision fails to generate positive returns, as investors deduce that near-term gains will not persist into future operating periods." (p. 18)

### Extract 20: Floropoulos (2018) — Selective Disclosure of Three-Year Horizon
> "The comparison between reports with upwards and downwards revisions shows that the disclosure of predictions for three or more years is associated with asymmetrical market responses that result in more favorable abnormal returns for the firm. Therefore, FY3 disclosures are more likely to be provided by analysts when these announcements offer positive news for future firm performance, a finding that suggests the existence of analyst bias." (p. 4)
